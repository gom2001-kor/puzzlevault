GridSmash Strategy: Leave Space and Plan Your Clears

February 25, 2026 · 5 min read · Updated September 26, 2026

GridSmash asks you to fit pieces on a ten-by-ten board while completing rows and columns. A good score grows out of a board that keeps accepting the pieces you receive. There is no score threshold that these habits can guarantee. A more useful aim for your next round is to finish each tray with at least one clear plan for the next placement.

1. Inspect all three pieces before placing one

The tray gives you three pieces, and a fresh tray arrives after all three have been used. Identify the least flexible shape first: a wide or awkward piece may have only one reasonable home, while a small piece can fit several gaps. You do not always need to place the largest piece first, but you should reserve its space before using a convenient location for something smaller.

Rotate a piece to compare its footprint. A short tap on a tray piece rotates it; keyboard players can select with 1–3, move the preview with arrow keys, rotate with R and place with Enter. The placement preview helps check both the shape and the rows or columns it would complete before committing.

2. Protect useful empty shapes

A scattered collection of single empty cells is less flexible than a connected patch that can hold different pieces. When two placements score the same, compare the spaces they leave. Does one preserve a long channel or a broad rectangle? Does the other leave a one-cell pocket that requires a particular piece to fill? Choose based on the remaining tray rather than following a fixed rule about always using corners.

A nearly full line is valuable only if you can reasonably fill its remaining cells. Avoid keeping several difficult gaps merely because they look close to completion. Sometimes clearing one ordinary row now opens more options than waiting for a dramatic crossing clear that the available pieces cannot support.

3. Understand what a streak rewards

Placed cells earn points, and complete rows and columns add line-clear points. The current line score starts at 100 times the number of lines completed together. Consecutive placements that clear lines build multipliers of 1, 1.5, 2, 3 and then 5. A placement with no completed line resets that streak. A tray refresh is not itself the unit used to count consecutive clears.

Look one placement beyond the immediate clear: after the filled cells disappear, can another remaining piece complete a line? That is a practical way to plan a streak. Do not sacrifice all your open space just to maintain it. A broken streak with a playable board can be a better outcome than a high multiplier followed by no legal placement.

4. Read the Shatter Zone countdown

Outside Zen, the bottom two rows become a Shatter Zone at the game’s ten-turn interval. Its countdown advances as trays are completed, and the zone lasts three of those turns. A placement that clears a row inside the active zone triples its line-clear score. When the zone expires, up to three empty cells in those rows fill with blocks.

This is a reason to monitor the bottom of the board, not to assume that every zone clear removes the eventual refill. The current expiration code still fills available cells when the countdown ends. Keep some alternative placement space above the zone, and compare the bonus opportunity with what the rest of your tray needs.

5. Use special pieces for a specific problem

A Crystal piece clears a nearby three-by-three area, which can open a blocked section but may also remove cells you intended to use in a line. Frost introduces frozen occupied cells in up to two empty locations; these need repeated line clearing before disappearing. A Wild piece occupies one cell and can complete an awkward gap. Read the board after an effect rather than assuming every special piece is automatically beneficial.

6. Review a decision, not a promised ranking

After a round, identify one placement that created a narrow pocket or removed a useful opening. Try a different choice next time and compare how long the tray remains playable. Daily mode can support discussions with friends about the same starting sequence; it does not establish a verified player percentile. You can also try HexMatch for a different kind of spatial planning.
